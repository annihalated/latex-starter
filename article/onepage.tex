\begin{abstract}
Tamil Nadu has built one of India's most effective public health systems through smart policies and strong administration. The state has controlled major diseases, reduced deaths in childbirth, and provided health insurance to millions. These successes show the state's ability to deliver results. The next stage of evolution is to create a modern institutional framework by updating public health law to make proven practices into permanent frameworks. Modern public health legislation would provide authority for systematic disease prevention, health monitoring, and coordinated policy implementation that administrative action alone cannot achieve. This legal foundation would transform Tamil Nadu's policy innovations into lasting institutional commitments, ensuring sustained health improvements and continued leadership in public health practice. 

\end{abstract}
\section{Proposal}
Tamil Nadu’s public health legal framework, anchored by the Tamil Nadu Public Health Act, 1939 (‘TNPHA’), stands as one of India’s earliest comprehensive public health laws. While groundbreaking for its time in establishing key institutions like the Public Health Board and recognising health’s connection to sectors like water and sanitation, this pre-independence framework is no longer aligned with Tamil Nadu's modern public health strategy. 

The TNPHA should be revised in accordance with the Directorate of Public Health and Preventive Medicine's current assessment of public health priorities. This can be done by ensuring that the statute
\begin{itemize}
    \item gives the Government the full range of modern legal tools to achieve those priorities
    \item has sufficient flexibility to allow for issuing rules and regulations to address future challenges and public health emergencies, so that repeated amendments are not required in emergencies, such as the Tamil Nadu Public Health (Second Amendment) Act, 2020
    \item creates adequate rights and safeguards for citizens while giving the Government adequate powers to achieve its public health goals
\end{itemize}

Any amendment process should also map and address the directorate's current priorities and pain points in terms of the administration of the \textsc{tnpha}. 

\section{Problem Statement}

The pre-independence legislation faces three key limitations that hamper its effectiveness in the present-day context:
    \subsection{Outdated regulatory approach} 
    
    The TNPHA relies on a penalty-based system targeting activities that no longer represent primary public health concerns: occupying houses with inadequate water supply, improper waste disposal in private spaces, library book use during illness, and failure to control rat populations. This approach reflects the public health priorities of the 1930s rather than evidence-based risk assessment.
    
    Modern public health legislation employs a broader range of regulatory tools. These include public warnings, disclosure requirements, health surveillance systems, and liability frameworks. Effective enforcement uses graduated responses—improvement notices and capacity-building programs before escalating to compensation requirements, closure orders, and penalties.

    \subsection{Imbalanced authority structure} 
    
    The legislation grants extensive discretionary powers to state authorities without corresponding safeguards or obligations. Contemporary public health law emphasizes balanced governance that establishes clear rights and obligations for both citizens and government. This includes mechanisms for informed consent, transparent decision-making processes, and citizen participation in health policy development.

    \subsection{Misalignment with current priorities} 
    
    The framework focuses predominantly on infectious disease control while Tamil Nadu's health challenges have shifted significantly. Non-communicable diseases now account for 70\% of deaths and 65\% of disability-adjusted life years in the state. With diabetes affecting over 40\% of households, the legal framework fails to address the state's primary health challenges.
    
    The disconnect is particularly evident when comparing the law's focus with Tamil Nadu's current health policies. Both the Tamil Nadu State Health Policy – Vision 2030 and the Tamil Nadu Health System Reform Program emphasize NCD prevention and management, yet the legal framework provides no foundation for these priorities.
